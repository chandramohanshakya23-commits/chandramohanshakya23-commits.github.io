Understanding ShakyaQX

How an open basin partitions its water.

The classical Budyko curve assumes a closed basin: all water comes from precipitation. Real basins also gain water from glaciers, aquifers and canals, and lose it to storage depletion and exports. This page shows, step by step and with interactive examples, how the SQX manifold handles those cases.

1 · What is an open hydrological system?

Precipitation is not the only water a basin has.

A closed basin receives water only as precipitation (P) and returns it as evaporation (E) and runoff (Q). An open basin also has a net storage or external term, ΔS. SQX writes this as an openness coordinate, φ = 1 + ΔS/P.

Switch views to see which flows the classical curve ignores.
Closed basin · classical Budyko
P = E + Q  →  E/P + Q/P = 1

All available water is precipitation, so φ = 1 always. E/P can never exceed 1, and storage changes are assumed to cancel out over time.

Open basin · SQX
P + ΔS = E + Q  →  E/P + Q/P = φ

Available water is P + ΔS, so φ = 1 + ΔS/P. When φ > 1, E/P and Q/P together can exceed 1, which the classical curve cannot represent.

φ > 1 · water-subsidized

Extra water from glaciers, aquifers or transfers. Examples: glacier-fed headwaters, canal-command areas.

φ = 1 · closed

Precipitation only. SQX reduces exactly to the classical Choudhury–Yang curve.

φ < 1 · water-constrained

Storage depletion or water export. Examples: drought years, donor basins in a transfer scheme.

Why dry periods are hard Published finding

The same storage change shifts φ by ΔS/P, so the shift grows sharply as P falls (∂φ/∂ΔS = 1/P). The published study identified this as the reason SQX is less reliable in low dry-season precipitation. The curve uses the ΔS you set above; the dot marks the drought-year P.

6 · Deeper insights

Where does each extra millimetre of water go?

The map below shows, for every combination of aridity x and openness φ, the share of a small extra water input that SQX sends to evaporation (∂(E/P)/∂φ). The rest becomes runoff. It follows directly from the equation, so it holds for any basin with the chosen γ.

Share of added water → E
0 · all to runoff1 · all to E
Higher γ makes the transition between the two regimes sharper.

The dashed line x = φ is where the two limits meet: E/P ≤ x (energy) and E/P ≤ φ (water). The solid white line marks an even 50/50 split. Well to the left, extra water mostly runs off; well to the right, it mostly evaporates.

01

The threshold moves with φ

Classically, the boundary between energy- and water-limited behaviour sits at x = 1. In SQX the limits are E/P ≤ x and E/P ≤ φ, so the boundary moves to x = φ. A subsidized basin stays energy-limited at a higher aridity.

02

Openness changes runoff more than ET in humid basins

Where x is small, nearly all of a subsidy or loss shows up in Q. That is why glacier melt and transfers matter most for river flow in wet, energy-limited regions.

03

In dry basins, extra water mostly evaporates

Where x is large, most added water is used by evaporation before it can reach the river. Transfers into arid regions raise ET far more than streamflow.

7 · Try it yourself

The equation in a few lines.

The SQX equation is published, so you can use it directly. Below are a Python function and a spreadsheet formula that reproduce every number on this page.

Python

def sqx(P, PET, dS, gamma=1.454):
    """Evaporative and runoff indices from the SQX manifold.
    Sign convention: dS > 0 adds water, dS < 0 removes it."""
    x = PET / P            # aridity index
    phi = 1 + dS / P       # openness coordinate
    if phi <= 0:
        raise ValueError("inadmissible: phi must be > 0")
    EP = x * (1 + (x / phi) ** gamma) ** (-1 / gamma)
    QP = phi - EP
    return {"x": x, "phi": phi, "E/P": EP, "Q/P": QP,
            "E": EP * P, "Q": QP * P}

print(sqx(1000, 800, 150))   # glacier-fed example

Reading these examples responsibly
  • All basins and numbers on this page are hypothetical. They demonstrate the equation, not a calibrated model of any real river.
  • γ is basin-specific. The value 1.454 is used here for consistency; real applications must calibrate it.
  • φ = 1 + ΔS/P is the operational form used in the published study. It becomes sensitive when P is small, as shown in the drought section.
  • Inter-basin transfers are shown as a uniform change in available water. Real schemes depend on where the water is delivered and how it is used.
  • SQX describes long-term partitioning. It is not a rainfall–runoff model for daily flows or floods.
